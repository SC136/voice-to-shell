Abstract—Internet of Things (IoT) deployment relies on com-
plex command-line interfaces that handle multiple commands,
diﬃcult to master, especially for nonexpert or novel technicians
and maintainers. Spoken dialog arises as the most natural
interaction for providing assistance to humans. Therefore, this
study presents a privacy-preserving multilingual voice assistant,
completely run on edge devices, that transforms natural language
instructions into executable commands for heterogeneous IoT
deployments. Two contexts are considered: Docker commands,
whose options and arguments are publicly accessible, and an
emergency luminaires system commands, a proprietary deploy-
ment whose information is not previously available to pretrained
models. Multiple datasets for both contexts have been drafted
to ﬁne-tune and evaluate proposed models. A benchmark and
its results for comparing proposed models are exposed, as well
as a functional implementation of the voice assistant, composed
of models from the comparison. The proposed system achieves
a 3.15 word error rate (WER) and 98.57% semantic similarity
for transcribing voice data, and takes only 2.8 s to generate a
rightful command with 81.36% accuracy and not needing GPU
acceleration, demonstrating reasonable performance on tested
edge devices. This study’s ﬁndings show that carefully ﬁne-tuned
small language models (SLMs) deliver high e ﬃciency for speech-
driven IoT device management at a fraction of the computational
cost, eliminating cloud dependence and protecting sensitive audio
data.
Index Terms —Assistant, command generation, edge, Internet
of Things (IoT), small language model (SLM), speech to text
(STT).

II. B ACKGROUND
In recent years, NLP techniques have represented a qualita-
tive leap in both research and application of AI [16]. Among
the di ﬀerent deep learning (DL) methods for solving NLP
tasks, the transformer architecture [17] rises as a ground-
breaking alternative by applying self-attention to process entire
sentences simultaneously rather than sequentially, enabling
obtaining state-of-the-art results. The popularity of this kind
of model has been increased by the appearance of several
libraries, which enable an easy setup for inference and training
and o ﬀer access to many pretrained models based on the
mentioned architecture.
Nowadays, there is a trend toward building larger models,
commonly called LLMs, targeting precision and accuracy of
results.3 Although they are highly promising, LLMs require
a vast amount of memory and computational resources, not

In this context, SLMs rise as cost-e ﬀective alternatives while
having less number of parameters (roughly from 100 M to
5 B) [12], [19]. They have reached similar accuracy results
compared to LLMs in reasoning and arithmetic tasks while
requiring only a fraction of the computational cost and oﬀering

Following this same trend toward increasingly compact archi-
tectures, recent proposals such as super tiny language models
(STLMs) [20] have emerged. An example is TinyStories [21],
which has demonstrated that a model with a single transformer
block and around 1-M parameters is capable of generating
coherent narratives in a speciﬁc domain. These proposals show
a growing interest in ultrasmall models to improve e ﬃciency
and enable their deployment in environments with limited
resources. However, data gathering and curation for training
and evaluation are critical steps when employing SLMs or
downsized alternatives, as many performance errors originate
from data quality issues [22]. This trend change is also aﬀected
by the gradual shift from cloud to edge computing driven by
privacy, latency, and sustainability requirements [23].
Data privacy protection is even more important when voice
data has to be stored or processed, as it is considered biometric
data by the GDPR. The voice assistant of this study is required
to ensure precision under real conditions and protection of
user voice data, while not depending on cloud infrastructures
and reducing latency, so user engagement is not a ﬀected [24].
Whisper [8] from OpenAI is an open-source STT model that
competently transcribes and translates multilingual audio data
into text, obtaining its Medium variant 7.5 and 6.35 word error
rate (WER) for transcribing English and Spanish audio data,
respectively, and 21.4% BLEU score for translating Spanish
audio data into English text. WER [25] measures how accurate
text predictions are compared to their ground truths, employing
the number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions. BLEU
[26] measures similarity between a predicted text and one or
more reference translations. Wav2Vec2 [9] and Conformer [27]
architectures, that appeared before Whisper, are quicker and
less complex approaches for STT which obtain ∼8 and ∼7
WER for transcribing English audio data, respectively. Some
variants of these architectures could be run on an edge device;
therefore, this study evaluates them in terms of performance
and robustness to be used as the STT required for a voice
assistant. It is essential to address its translation capabilities in
order to be deployed in a multilingual production environment,
as assistants are likely to achieve higher WER when processing
non-English audio data [28]. To mitigate potential language-
induced errors, previous studies suggest ﬁx or correct obtained
texts from STT models to reduce hallucinations and WER,
even in low-resource languages [29].
There is a growing trend toward automating IoT deploy-
ments through the application of NLP techniques. EcoMate
[30] integrates GPT-3.54 with Home Assistant 5 to transform a
given input into Y AML routines ready for execution, achieving
an accuracy of about 70%, thus simplifying the interaction

Turbo from OpenAI API6 to generate “trigger–action” rules in

the text→ action challenge in IoT environments and demon-

general-purpose nature, reinforcing the relevance of adopting
a local, domain-trained, and speciﬁcity-oriented approach such
as the one proposed in this study.
Mastering command-line syntax involves a high cognitive
load and e ﬀective mechanisms to reduce human error and
learning time [1]. In order to address these issues, several
research studies focus on employing NLP techniques to assist
users with command-line interfaces. AST-Transformer [35]
links user requests with relevant fragments of the user man-
ual ( man Linux command), facilitating the explanation of
commonly used Bash commands and “teaching” rather than
automatically executing. Project CLAI [36] encapsulates a set
of tools (generic Bash command correction, example retrieval,
and documentation) to provide suggestions to the user without
exiting the shell. Segmented invocation transformer (SIT)
[37] optimizes Bash command generation by incorporating
constituency trees.
Few literature studies have completed the complete audio→
text→ action pipeline. A recent framework for 5G networks
[38] combines cognitive machine reasoning with NLP, which
processes natural language instructions, potentially dictated
by voice, and transforms them into Y AML /JSON descriptors
that an orchestrator can deploy without human intervention.

the subject of this study it reduce service provisioning time
and improve network metrics. However, it requires powerful
hardware and constant connectivity, making it feasible to be
deployed in embedded IoT gateways. Related to this study
domain, AssistantGPT [39] is capable of translating spoken
or written commands into OS calls over 90% success rate.
Nonetheless, it is entirely dependent on the OpenAI API,
which entails continuous connectivity, exposure to sensitive
data, variable costs, and no awareness of proprietary com-
mands. Regarding studies that seem to include an agentic
workﬂow, AutoGen [40] holds several agents acting as clas-
siﬁers, deciding which agent should deliver the user request,
rather than directly executing the requested action. HERA [41]

III. M ETHODS
A. System Overview
This study proposes a privacy-preserving voice assistant
pipeline to be run on edge devices and used by IoT deployment
maintenance sta ﬀ. The workﬂow of the proposed system is
shown in Fig. 1. Instructions are captured by the device’s
embedded microphone in the form of audio data. Traditional
input devices, as keyboards, are also supported as fallback
interface options. Audio data is then either transcribed or
translated to English, if needed, by an STT model, conﬁgured
with the user’s language. This setup enables multilingual
functionality. Then, the obtained text from system interfaces
would be processed by an agentic subsystem, although not
implemented within this work, in order to extend user requests
to produce high-quality prompts with deployment information
and context for the following components. This study focuses
on building the audio→ text→ action workﬂow, leaving the
mentioned subsystem undetermined, thus evaluating the rest of
the components in a standalone manner. As previous research
suggests [29], a spell checker (FIX) trained on domain-speciﬁc
transcripts is proposed to reduce potential hallucinations and
errors produced by the STT component. Once a curated
text is obtained, the text to command (TTC) component,
trained on domain-speciﬁc data, produces a command ready
to be executed on a terminal. However, human intervention is
required to conﬁrm the execution of the given command [43].
In order to build STT, FIX, and TTC components of the
proposed system, this study.
1) Datasets comprising (prompt, command) samples are
built to train the TTC component. As TTC is context-
dependent, two datasets are formed: one for Docker
(public) and the other for the emergency luminaires
system (proprietary). Datasets are split into train, val-
idation, and test data for training and evaluation.
2) V oice data of human subjects enunciating prompt sam-
ples from test splits of previously mentioned datasets is
recorded to build audio datasets, so STT performance is
evaluated.
3) Datasets comprising (input, target) samples are build to
train FIX components. Data is obtained from STT errors.
Datasets are split into train, validation, and test data for
training and evaluation.
4) A benchmark for evaluating and comparing proposed
models is designed employing well-known standard
metrics.
5) A use case to implement this study system is proposed
and conducted.

C. Data
1) TTC Datasets: Related research highlights the impor-
tance of ﬁne-tuning with small, balanced, high-quality,
domain-speciﬁc datasets to e ﬀectively obtain accurate pro-
prietary commands and reducing hallucinations and incorrect
predictions [14], [22], [52]. Thus, two hyper-specialized text to
text datasets, for Docker and the emergency luminaires system,
are built within this study.
To build the Docker dataset, broadly used for IoT deploy-
ments on the edge [13], 133 relevant command options have
been manually selected (i.e., image, container, volume,
etc.) and 24 prompt samples for each one have been manually
drafted, yielding a total of 3192 (prompt, command) samples.
Each sample has been created with reference to the o ﬃcial
user manual and tested in an isolated environment to check
that the syntax and behavior are correct. Some precautions
have been taken in order to reduce potential generation
errors: redundant aliases removal (i.e., docker images and
docker image ls ), and concrete identiﬁers usage instead
of generic placeholders (i.e., python instead of image).
The outcome is a compact, consistent, and functionally robust
corpus, manually veriﬁed, that provides a solid foundation for
reliable specialization of lightweight models in real container
administration tasks. As an example, the prompt “show how
much disk space the Ubuntu container is using” is paired with
the command docker inspect -s ubuntu .
As a proprietary service, emergency luminaires system com-
mands present a challenge to previous strategies. No language
model has ever been trained on this particular set of commands
or any information about this speciﬁc deployment. These com-
mands have to be memorized entirely by technicians, being an
impractical and costly task that would considerably lengthen
common diagnostic and maintenance operations. In practice,
the command-line is accessed by two distinct roles: installers
and maintainers. Installers are the professionals responsible
for the initial deployment of the system, while maintainers

D. Training
In this study, 20 transformer models are trained: 6 text to

A. TTC Component
Several models have been evaluated for composing the TTC
component of this study proposed system for a voice assis-

process, while also providing comparative benchmark met-
rics that would guide the selection of the most appropriate
model according to hardware constraints in edge environments.
Evaluation results are shown in Table V. EQ results show
the generated commands that could be directly executed on
a terminal, while LEV results report how similar predicted
commands are to their ground truths.
All models beneﬁt from being trained on the custom datasets
exposed in this study, as none of them are able to predict the
rightful command given a proper instruction before training.
Even more, their generated commands are only ∼18% similar
at most to what they should be. Regarding Docker command
context, T5 Large achieves the best results with 92.86%
exact matches and 99.10% text similarity. Overall, text to
text models obtain 86.28% mean exact matches while text
generation models only achieve 55.89%. These results demon-
strate that text to text models sequential input–output formula
aligns perfectly with the task of transforming natural language
instructions into commands. Similar results are achieved on
the luminaires command context, in which text to text and text
generation models obtain 92.29% and 46.31% exact matches,
respectively, with T5 variants as the best performers. Among
the text to text models proposed in this study, T5 variants seem
to ﬁt slightly better to the TTC task in comparison with its
derived model, Flan T5, with 90.09% and 88.48% mean exact
matches, respectively. In general, the T5 family demonstrates
outstanding performance for this task, not only with its larger
variants but also the more compact ones, surpassing 75% of
exact matches with at least half of the parameters of text
generation models, highlighting its suitability for resource-
constrained environments without compromising precision.
While they are not the quickest, T5 variants also achieve
suitable inference times on CPU-only devices, with T5 Small
and T5 Base obtaining 0.20 and 0.43 s, respectively. T5
Large, considering its greater size, achieves 1.82 s of inference
time, yet acceptable for real-time applications. In contrast,
larger models could not be executed on CPU-only edge hard-
ware under the tested conditions, as their computational and
memory requirements exceed the capabilities of such devices
without GPU acceleration. Although the comparison between
text to text and text generation models has already been
addressed, this study results demonstrate that employing larger
SLMs or even LLMs for the TTC component does not guar-
antee better results in comparison with smaller approaches,
demonstrating that the number of learnable parameters and /or
FLOPS have an impact on performance across the same model
architecture, but not across di ﬀerent ones. Including them in
the comparative aims to highlight the di ﬀerences in perfor-
mance for command generation and to ensure that smaller
alternatives are suitable to run on the edge and still obtain high
accuracy.
The evaluation of the TTC component highlights the impact
of model size, computational cost, and command generation
accuracy for designing SLMs for edge applications under

V. C ONCLUSION
This study focuses on the following research question: How
eﬀective are ﬁne-tuned SLMs combined with embedded STT
models in generating accurate commands for IoT infrastruc-
ture maintenance by nonnative English speakers in o ﬄine,
resource-constrained environments? In order to address it, this
study evaluates di ﬀerent state-of-the-art STT, text to text , and
text generation models with the objective of designing a cost-
eﬀective privacy-preserving voice assistant locally run on edge
devices. The assistant would aid IoT solutions technicians in
usual deployment or maintenance operations. The proposed
system for the voice assistant, as outlined in Fig. 1, include
three components: STT, for transcribing and translating into
English given audio data into text; FIX ( spell checker ), for
correcting spell errors found in transcripts obtained from
the STT component; and TTC, to transform a given textual
instruction into a command ready to be executed. In order
to build them, several datasets have been manually drafted,
as described in Section III-C, for training or evaluating the
proposed models. In Sections IV-A and IV-B, a benchmark and
its results for comparing the proposed models with di ﬀerent
standard and well-known metrics are exposed. A functional
implementation of this study’s proposed use case has been
addressed in Section IV-C by employing models from the
previous evaluation process. This voice assistant for command
generation would obtain a rightful command from a given
instruction in 2.80 s, without needing to use any GPU, with
∼81% accuracy. If any GPU is available in the target edge
environment, a more powerful STT model could be employed,
and this system’s accuracy would increase up to ∼85%,
obtaining the generated command in only 0.85 s. This solution
oﬀers a reasonable inference time and resource usage for a
local privacy-preserving solution.
This study’s ﬁndings show that carefully ﬁne-tuned SLMs
can deliver high e ﬃciency for speech-driven command gen-

eration in IoT deployment maintenance. However, further
challenges and future work must be addressed. Language and
accent barriers yet exist for STT models, being more precise
at processing native speakers’ voice data, even more so if
they speak standard accents. Broader multilingual datasets
that gather spontaneous and noisy audio data from nonstan-
dard accents are required to ﬁne-tune STT models so their
robustness is increased. This study evaluated text to text and
text generation models to generate commands from a given
instruction, although only two command contexts have been
tested. The proposed voice assistant should o ﬀer aid with
other IoT tools and environments, such as Kubernetes or
LoRaW AN, thus requiring datasets comprising samples from
those. Moreover, building the complete proposed agent-based
system will enable providing deployment context to given

niques to search for command information, and, in general,
a conversational assistant capable of reasoning and handling
long and even ambiguous instructions. Finally, user acceptance
and usability studies must be conducted on the ﬁnal system,
although users are expected to conﬁrm each generated com-
